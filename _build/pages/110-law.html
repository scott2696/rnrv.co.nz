The position in one paragraph

The short version

You are not breaking the law. It has never been an offence for a New Zealander to play at an offshore online casino, and the Online Casino Gambling Act 2026 did not change that — it regulates operators, not players. What is changing is which operators may lawfully serve you. From 1 December 2026, an operator needs a New Zealand licence or a pending application to continue. Fifteen licences exist, allocated by auction. Some sites you use today will exit the market. Withdraw your balances rather than leaving them.

What the Online Casino Gambling Act 2026 actually does

The Act creates New Zealand's first dedicated licensing regime for online casino gambling. Three features matter most.

It has extraterritorial reach

The Act applies to any operator enabling online casino gambling from New Zealand, wherever that operator is incorporated. An operator in Curaçao, Anjouan or Malta that accepts New Zealand customers is within its scope. This is the provision that makes the regime enforceable against a market that is almost entirely offshore.

It caps the number of licences

Fifteen licences, allocated through a competitive auction rather than granted on application to anyone meeting a standard. Each covers a single brand; no applicant may hold more than three; each runs up to three years with a possible five-year renewal.

It restricts advertising severely

Only licensed operators may advertise online casino gambling to New Zealanders, and even then several whole categories of advertising are prohibited. Because no licences have been issued yet, all online casino advertising to New Zealanders has been barred since 1 May 2026.

It is supported by two instruments: the Online Casino Gambling Regulations 2026 (in force 3 July 2026), whose Part 4 contains the advertising rules, and the Online Casino Gambling (Minimum Standards) 2026 (in force 8 July 2026), which set out the operational requirements a licensee must meet.

The fifteen licences

How many, and for how long

Fifteen licences. Each valid for up to three years, with the possibility of renewal for a further five. Each covers a single brand.

How they are allocated

By competitive auction, run through GETS. Operators first submitted an expression of interest; accepted EOIs proceeded to the auction; successful bidders may then apply for a licence.

Concentration limit

No single applicant may be granted more than three licences. That prevents one group taking the market, though fifteen brands could still mean as few as five operators.

What an application requires

A business plan, plus detailed strategies covering advertising and marketing, consumer protection, harm prevention and minimisation, and regulatory compliance. Assessed together with the EOI material to determine suitability.

The practical consequence of a capped, auctioned market is concentration. A fifteen-brand market is a very different landscape from the effectively unlimited Curaçao licensing that characterises the operators on this site today. Whether that produces better consumer outcomes or merely fewer choices is the open question in New Zealand gambling policy.

Advertising restrictions — among the tightest in the region

Section 10 of the Act permits an online casino gambling advertisement directed at New Zealanders only if it is published in accordance with a licence, and the gambling is not substantially similar to, likely to compete with, or involve betting on a protected New Zealand lottery.

Because no licences have been issued, operators have been barred from advertising online casino gambling to New Zealand customers since 1 May 2026.

Permitted methods, once licensed

  • Broadcasting
  • Video on-demand content
  • Display in public view at premises or a place
  • Physical publication
  • Direct communication to a person
  • Online or within software
  • At an event, in certain circumstances — for example where the event relates to online casino gambling

Restrictions on those permitted methods

  • No advertising during a live broadcast, or within 30 minutes before or after
  • No advertising on public transport
  • No advertising on the front page of a multi-page publication

Categories prohibited outright

  • Advertisements using sponsorships, endorsements or affiliate arrangements
  • Advertisements using inducements or interactive features
  • Advertisements promoting impulsivity or urgency
  • False or misleading advertisements
  • Personalised advertisements
  • Advertisements containing certain sensory cues
  • Advertisements portraying alcohol, violence, denigration or discrimination
  • Advertisements causing offence
  • Advertisements referring to community contributions
  • Advertisements breaching regulation 44 (age requirements) or regulation 45 (harm minimisation messages)
  • Advertisements involving other types of gambling

Enforcement so far

The DIA has not waited for the licensing regime to become operational before acting.

It has issued infringement notices to multiple social media influencers who promoted offshore casino services to New Zealand audiences, and to the offshore operator itself. Three features of that enforcement are worth noting because they indicate how the regime will be applied:

  • The DIA treated each advertisement as a separate contravention, so one influencer accumulated repeated penalties across several posts.
  • It maintained ongoing monitoring of the offending accounts rather than acting once.
  • It directed social media platforms and internet service providers to take down the illegal advertising.

That is an active regulator with practical tools, and it is a material change from the pre-2026 position in which offshore operators marketed into New Zealand with little friction.

What this means for you as a New Zealand player

  1. You are not committing an offence

    There is no penalty in New Zealand law for a player who gambles at an offshore online casino. The Act creates obligations for operators and for advertisers. No provision creates a player offence, and none has been proposed.

  2. Some sites will disappear from the New Zealand market

    Fifteen licences against a market currently served by hundreds of offshore brands means most will exit. An operator that has not applied must cease by 1 December 2026.

  3. Withdraw your balances

    The single most practical step. Do not leave money in an account at an operator that may stop serving New Zealand. Treat a casino account as a wallet for the session, not a place to store funds.

  4. Complete your verification now

    If a site exits and you need to withdraw quickly, an unverified account is the worst possible position to be in. Upload ID, proof of address and proof of payment method at every site where you hold funds.

  5. Expect better protection eventually

    The Minimum Standards and the consumer-protection requirements in licence applications should deliver player protections the offshore regime does not offer — most importantly around fund protection. That is a 2027 proposition, not a current one.

  6. Keep records

    Deposits, withdrawals, balances and account correspondence. If an operator exits messily, contemporaneous records are the only useful thing you will have.

How betting law differs — and why the confusion matters

New Zealand's two gambling regimes are moving in opposite directions simultaneously, which produces a lot of incorrect information.

The two regimes compared as at September 2026.

The policy logic behind the divergence is funding. Betting turnover pays for the New Zealand racing industry and for national sporting organisation grants, so money bet offshore represented a direct loss to a domestic sector. Online casino turnover funded nothing domestically either way, so the policy question there was whether to regulate and tax it rather than whether to protect an incumbent.
