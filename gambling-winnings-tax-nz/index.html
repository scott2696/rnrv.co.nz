Tax on Gambling Winnings NZ

For almost every New Zealander the answer is a clean no: gambling winnings are the proceeds of chance, not income, and there is nothing to declare. But two exceptions genuinely catch people — gambling carried on as a business, and the disposal of cryptocurrency you won. Both are explained here, with the tests Inland Revenue actually applies.

The short answer

If you are a recreational gambler in New Zealand, you pay no tax on your winnings and you declare nothing. Pokies, casino games, online casino, sports betting, racing, Lotto — all of it. Any amount. Inland Revenue treats gambling winnings as the proceeds of chance, not as income.

Two exceptions exist: gambling carried on as a business, and the disposal of cryptocurrency. Both are set out below.

Why New Zealand does not tax gambling winnings

New Zealand's income tax system taxes income, and income has a settled meaning: something that comes in with sufficient regularity and from a source that produces it. A windfall — a lottery win, a found sum, a gift, a gambling win — is a receipt of capital in nature rather than income.

Gambling winnings are treated as the proceeds of chance. The player is not carrying on a business, is not providing a service, and has no reasonable expectation of profit. Over any sufficient run, the player loses; the occasional win is a chance outcome, not a return on an activity.

The logic runs both ways, which is why it is stable: because winnings are not income, losses are not deductible. If the state taxed wins, it would face claims for the far larger aggregate of losses.

The two exceptions

1. Gambling as a business

If gambling amounts to a business carried on for profit — with system, scale, regularity and a realistic expectation of profit — the proceeds can be taxable as business income. A high bar, and one that catches very few people. Full test below.

2. Cryptoasset disposals

The gambling win itself is untaxed. But Inland Revenue treats crypto as property, so disposing of coins you won — selling, swapping, or spending them — can create a taxable event on any gain in value since you received them. Full explanation below.

The professional gambler test

There is no single statutory test and no bright line. Inland Revenue and the courts assess the overall character of the activity, weighing a number of factors together.

Factors considered in assessing whether gambling amounts to a business. No single factor is decisive; the assessment is of the pattern as a whole.
FactorPoints towards recreationalPoints towards business
ScaleModest stakes relative to incomeTurnover that would be commercial in any other context
FrequencyOccasional or weeklyDaily, sustained over years
Time devotedEvenings and weekendsFull-time, displacing other work
System or skillReliance on chance; games with no available edgeAn identifiable, applied edge — modelling, arbitrage, advantage play
Record-keepingNone, or casualDetailed, business-like, used to manage the activity
Reliance on the incomeOther employment or income supports youGambling is the main or only income source
Expectation of profitPlaying for entertainment; expecting to lose overallA genuine, evidenced expectation of profit over time

Where this line actually falls

The game matters enormously. In pokies, roulette or any RNG casino game, no edge is mathematically available — the house edge is built into the paytable and cannot be overcome by skill. It is very difficult to argue a business exists in an activity with a guaranteed negative expected value.

Poker is different, because it is played against other players rather than against the house, and a skilled player can have a genuine long-run edge. That is why the small number of New Zealanders who have faced this question have generally been poker players.

Sports betting sits in between: an edge is theoretically available, but bookmakers limit or close accounts that demonstrate one, which tends to prevent the scale a business requires.

If several rows in the table above describe you, take advice. A New Zealand chartered accountant will resolve it in one conversation, and the cost of getting it wrong in either direction is higher than the cost of asking.

Cryptocurrency winnings: the exception most people miss

This section exists because we could not find a single competitor page in the New Zealand market that covers it, and it applies to a growing number of players as crypto cashiers become the default fast-withdrawal route.

The two questions, separated

Question one: is the win taxable? No. A gambling win is the proceeds of chance whether it arrives as New Zealand dollars or as USDT.

Question two: is the crypto disposal taxable? Possibly. Inland Revenue treats cryptoassets as property, not currency. Disposing of property can produce a taxable gain. Disposal includes:

  • Selling crypto for New Zealand dollars
  • Swapping one cryptoasset for another
  • Using crypto to pay for goods or services
  • Gifting it in some circumstances

What happens after you win

The win is untaxed. What you do with the money afterwards may not be.

  • Interest on the winnings in a savings account or term deposit is taxable income, with resident withholding tax deducted at source. Give your bank your IRD number or you will be taxed at the no-declaration rate.
  • Dividends from shares bought with the winnings are taxable income.
  • Rental income from a property bought with the winnings is taxable income, with the usual deduction rules.
  • Foreign investment may bring you within the foreign investment fund rules depending on the amount, which is worth checking above roughly NZ$50,000 of overseas holdings.
  • Gifting the money attracts no gift duty — gift duty was abolished in New Zealand in 2011 — though very large gifts can have implications for residential care subsidies and relationship property.

None of this makes the win taxable. It simply means a large win moves you into ordinary investment taxation, which is worth a conversation with an accountant if the sum is significant.

The common design across most of these countries is the same: tax the operator rather than the player. It is simpler to administer, it captures revenue at the point of profit, and it avoids the losses-deduction problem entirely. New Zealand's new online casino licensing regime follows that pattern.

Records worth keeping

If you are clearly recreational

Nothing is required for tax. Keeping a simple record of deposits and withdrawals is still worthwhile — for managing your own spending, not for Inland Revenue. Most people materially underestimate what they have staked over a year.

If you won in cryptocurrency

Date, coin, quantity and NZD value at receipt, and the same at disposal. This is the only record that determines whether a gain exists. Capture the receipt side yourself — casinos do not provide it.

If you might be near the professional line

Everything. Every stake, every result, time devoted, methodology, and any staking arrangements. Contemporaneous records are the only useful evidence if the question is ever asked, and reconstructed ones are not.

Frequently asked questions

Do you pay tax on gambling winnings in New Zealand?

No, not as a recreational player. Inland Revenue treats gambling winnings as the proceeds of chance rather than income, so they are not taxable and there is nothing to include on your IR3. This applies to pokies, casino table games, online casino play, sports betting, racing and Lotto, and it applies regardless of the amount. A NZ$500,000 jackpot is as untaxed as a NZ$50 win.

Do I need to declare casino winnings to IRD?

No. If the winnings are not taxable there is nothing to declare and no form to complete. You do not need to tell Inland Revenue about a win, and receiving one does not create a filing obligation.

Where you may still want records is if you are near the professional-gambler line, or if you won in cryptocurrency and will later dispose of it — in both cases contemporaneous records matter. Keeping them costs nothing.

When is gambling taxable in New Zealand?

When it amounts to a business. Income tax applies to income from a business carried on for profit, and in rare cases gambling meets that description.

Inland Revenue and the courts look at the overall picture rather than any single factor: the scale and frequency of the activity, whether there is a system or method rather than chance, whether the person relies on it for income, the time devoted to it, whether records are kept in a business-like way, and whether there is a genuine intention and realistic prospect of profit.

The bar is high and it is deliberately hard to cross. In practice it catches a small number of professional poker players and almost nobody else. Someone who bets every weekend and wins more than they lose is still, in the ordinary case, a recreational gambler.

Am I a professional gambler for tax purposes?

Almost certainly not, but the question is worth taking seriously if several of these apply to you: gambling is your main source of income; you devote most of your working time to it; you operate to a system with an identifiable edge rather than relying on chance; you keep detailed records as a business would; you bet at a scale that would be commercial in any other context; and you have a realistic expectation of profit over time.

One or two of those does not make you a professional. The pattern taken together might. If it describes you, get advice from a New Zealand chartered accountant rather than relying on a comparison site — including this one.

Do I pay tax on crypto casino winnings in NZ?

Two separate questions with different answers, and this is where most people go wrong.

The gambling win is not taxed. Winning 1,000 USDT at a casino is the proceeds of chance, exactly like winning NZ$1,600 in cash.

But Inland Revenue treats cryptoassets as property, not currency. Disposing of crypto — selling it for New Zealand dollars, swapping it for another coin, or using it to buy something — can create a taxable event on any gain in value between when you acquired it and when you disposed of it.

So winning 0.05 BTC and selling it the same day involves essentially no gain. Winning 0.05 BTC and selling it six months later after a price rise raises a separate question about that gain. See the full section below.

Is there GST on gambling winnings?

Not for you as a player. GST obligations in gambling sit with operators, not customers. A New Zealander receiving winnings has no GST consequence, and an offshore operator's GST position is not your concern.

Do I pay tax on a big jackpot in New Zealand?

No. There is no threshold above which gambling winnings become taxable for a recreational player. A seven-figure progressive jackpot is treated identically to a NZ$20 win: the proceeds of chance, not income, not taxable, nothing to declare.

What does become relevant at that scale is what you do with the money afterwards. Interest earned on it is taxable income. Dividends from shares bought with it are taxable. Rental income from a property bought with it is taxable. The win is untaxed; the returns it subsequently generates are not.

What about winnings from an overseas online casino?

The same treatment. It does not matter where the operator is licensed — Curaçao, Anjouan, Malta or, from 2027, New Zealand. What matters is that the winnings are the proceeds of chance for a recreational gambler, which makes them untaxed.

The Online Casino Gambling Act 2026 and the DIA licensing regime do not change the tax treatment of player winnings. They regulate operators. See our NZ online casino law guide.

Can I claim gambling losses as a tax deduction?

No, and the logic is consistent. Because gambling winnings are not taxable income for a recreational player, gambling losses are not deductible expenses. The two sides match.

A professional gambler whose winnings are taxable as business income would, in principle, deduct associated losses and expenses — but that is the direct consequence of being in the taxable category, not an advantage available to anyone else.

Do I pay tax if I win betting on sports?

No, as a recreational bettor. Sports and racing betting winnings are the proceeds of chance and are not taxable, whether the bet is placed with TAB NZ or with an offshore bookmaker.

The professional exception applies here as it does elsewhere, and sports betting is one of the areas where someone might plausibly cross the line — a bettor operating a genuine model at scale, full time, with an identifiable edge. Rare, but less rare than in casino games where no edge is available.

What records should I keep?

If you are plainly recreational: none are required. Keeping them anyway is good practice for managing your own spending, which is a better reason than tax.

If you won in cryptocurrency: record the date, the coin, the quantity and the NZD value at the moment you received it, and the same at the moment you dispose of it. That is what determines whether a gain exists. Most exchanges export disposal records; casinos generally do not, so capture the acquisition side yourself at the time.

If you are near the professional line: keep everything — every stake, every result, time devoted, and any methodology. If the question is ever asked, contemporaneous records are the only useful evidence.

What is the tax rate on gambling winnings in New Zealand?

Nil, for a recreational player. There is no gambling winnings tax rate in New Zealand because the winnings are not treated as income in the first place — they are the proceeds of chance. There is no threshold, no withholding, and nothing to declare, whatever the amount.

The exceptions are gambling carried on as a business, and separately the disposal of cryptocurrency you won, since Inland Revenue treats cryptoassets as property. Both are covered above.

Why are gambling winnings not taxed in New Zealand?

Because New Zealand taxes income, and a gambling win is not income in the relevant sense. Income comes in with regularity from a source that produces it; a windfall does not. A recreational gambler is not carrying on a business, provides no service, and has no reasonable expectation of profit — over any sufficient run they lose.

The logic is also what makes it stable: because winnings are not taxable, losses are not deductible. If the state taxed wins it would face claims against the far larger aggregate of losses. Most comparable countries reach the same conclusion and tax the operator instead, which is the approach New Zealand's new online casino licensing regime takes.

Are lottery winnings taxed in New Zealand?

No. A Lotto, Powerball or Instant Kiwi win is treated the same way as any other gambling win: the proceeds of chance, not income, not taxable, and nothing to declare. That holds for a Powerball jackpot as much as for a NZ$20 win.

What becomes taxable is what the money then earns. Interest on it is taxable income with resident withholding tax deducted at source, dividends from shares bought with it are taxable, and so is rental income from property. Gift duty was abolished in 2011, so sharing a win with family attracts no gift duty — though very large gifts can affect residential care subsidies and relationship property. Above roughly NZ$50,000 of overseas holdings, the foreign investment fund rules may also apply.
