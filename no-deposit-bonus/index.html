Why the offer still has some value

The expected-value calculation above is dominated by the average case, where you clear nothing. The actual value sits in the tail: occasionally one of those 20 spins lands a 200× hit, the balance jumps to NZ$40, and 50× wagering on that becomes a real but achievable NZ$2,000 of turnover. That is why a conversion cap exists — it truncates precisely the outcome that makes the offer worth anything.

Net of everything: expect NZ$1 to NZ$4 of value, mostly as entertainment rather than cash. Claim it to look around the site. Do not build a plan around it.

How to claim a no deposit bonus properly

  1. Register with accurate details

    Your real name, real date of birth, real address. These must match the identity documents you will supply at withdrawal, and a mismatch voids the bonus and any winnings. There is no upside to being approximate here.

  2. Verify email or phone as prompted

    This is usually what triggers the credit. Check spam if nothing arrives within a few minutes.

  3. Read the terms before you spin

    Wagering multiple, what it applies to, maximum bet while active, expiry, conversion cap, and critically whether a qualifying deposit is required before a first withdrawal. That last term is present more often than not and it changes the whole proposition.

  4. Check whether the spins are locked to one game

    Almost always yes — Book of the Fallen in Lucky7even's case. You cannot choose a higher-RTP title.

  5. Note the expiry

    Typically 7 days for the spins and 7 to 30 days for wagering on the winnings. Missing either forfeits the balance.

  6. Complete KYC before you try to withdraw

    If a win does clear, verification will be required and it will be thorough. Doing it in advance removes a delay at the only moment the offer has produced anything.

Beware the number in the headline

Spin count is the easiest figure for an operator to inflate, because spin value is set separately. "100 free spins" at NZ$0.10 a spin is worth exactly half of "50 free spins" at NZ$0.20. Always check the per-spin value, which is in the terms rather than the banner.

No deposit bonus max cashout explained, and "keep what you win"

Two terms decide whether a no deposit bonus ever becomes money.

Maximum cashout

A hard ceiling on what bonus-derived winnings can convert to withdrawable cash, typically NZ$50 to NZ$200 on a no-deposit offer. It is the term that removes the only outcome that makes these offers worth anything — the rare large spin win. If you hit NZ$900 from 20 free spins and the cap is NZ$100, you withdraw NZ$100.

"Keep what you win"

A no deposit offer with no wagering on the winnings, so whatever you win is immediately withdrawable. This is the genuinely valuable version and it is correspondingly rare — usually paired with a very small spin count, a low cap, or both.

Do not confuse it with "no wagering on the spins", which is a much weaker claim and sometimes means only that the spin stake is free while the winnings still carry a multiple. Read which one is stated.

Frequently asked questions

What is a no deposit bonus?

Bonus credit or free spins awarded before you fund an account, usually after you register and verify your email or phone number. You play with the casino's money and, subject to terms, keep some of what you win.

The operator's purpose is acquisition: a verified account with a real email address and a demonstrated willingness to play is worth more than the few dollars the offer costs. That is a fair exchange as long as you understand what you are exchanging.

Which NZ casinos actually have a no deposit bonus?

Genuine, verifiable no-deposit offers for New Zealanders are rare. As at September 2026, the one we can confirm on our own list is Lucky7even's 20 free spins on Book of the Fallen, awarded after email verification and carrying 50x wagering.

Several other operators run intermittent no-deposit promotions through email campaigns or seasonal offers rather than as a standing feature. Rather than pad this page with offers we cannot verify, we list what we have confirmed and say plainly that the rest come and go. If a site is advertising a large permanent no-deposit bonus for NZ, read the terms very carefully before assuming it is what it appears to be.

Are no deposit bonuses really free?

Free of money, not free of cost. You give a verified email address, a phone number, and — at the point you want to withdraw anything — full identity documents. You also enter a marketing funnel designed to convert you into a depositing customer, which is the entire purpose of the offer.

That is a reasonable trade if you were considering the site anyway. It is a poor trade if you are collecting no-deposit offers across many sites, because you end up with your identity documents held by a dozen offshore operators for a total expected return of a few dollars.

What is a no deposit bonus actually worth?

Less than it looks, and the arithmetic is straightforward. Take 20 free spins at NZ$0.20 a spin: NZ$4 of stake. At 96% RTP, expected winnings are about NZ$3.84. Apply 50x wagering to those winnings — NZ$192 of required turnover — and at 96% RTP you would expect to lose about NZ$7.70 generating it, which is more than the winnings.

The realistic cash value of a typical 20-spin no-deposit offer is roughly NZ$1 to NZ$4, and most of the time it is zero because the balance is gone before the wagering is met. Occasionally a spin lands a large win and the conversion cap becomes the binding constraint instead. Treat it as a free look at the lobby, not as income.

Why is the wagering on no deposit bonuses so high?

Because there is no deposit to offset the operator's cost. On a deposit match, the casino has your money and expects to hold a margin on the turnover you generate with it. On a no-deposit offer it has nothing, so the only protection against straightforward extraction is a wagering requirement high enough to make extraction unprofitable.

That is why 50x is standard on no-deposit offers against 35–40x on deposit bonuses, and why the conversion cap is usually tight as well.

Can I actually withdraw no deposit bonus winnings?

Yes, in principle, if you complete the wagering and stay within the conversion cap. In practice it is uncommon, because the wagering usually exhausts the balance first.

Where people do win, the conversion cap bites: a NZ$100 cap on a NZ$900 win means you withdraw NZ$100. You will also need to complete full KYC before any withdrawal, and many operators require a qualifying deposit before processing a first withdrawal from a no-deposit bonus. That last term catches almost everyone — check whether it applies before you invest time in clearing.

What is a keep what you win bonus?

A no-deposit offer with no wagering on the winnings, so what you win is immediately withdrawable. It is the genuinely valuable form of the product and it is correspondingly rare — usually a very small number of spins, a low cap, or both.

Do not confuse "keep what you win" with "no wagering on the spins", which is a different and much weaker claim that sometimes means the spin value is free but winnings still carry a multiple. Read which is stated.

Do no deposit bonuses require verification?

To claim: usually email or SMS verification only. To withdraw: full KYC every time — photo ID, proof of address, and often proof of a payment method, which can mean making a deposit you were trying to avoid.

Operators run these checks because no-deposit offers attract multi-accounting. Expect a single offer per person, per household and per IP address, strictly enforced, with winnings voided where duplicates are detected.

Are free spins the same as a no deposit bonus?

Free spins are one form of it. The other is bonus credit — a small cash-equivalent balance, say NZ$10, that you can spend across the lobby rather than on one named title.

Credit is more flexible and usually carries similar wagering. Free spins are tied to a specific pokie at a specific stake, which means the operator controls the RTP and volatility of how you use the offer. Neither is meaningfully better; both are small.

Is a no deposit bonus worth claiming at all?

Sometimes, and for one good reason: it lets you test a site's registration flow, lobby, mobile experience and support before committing money. That reconnaissance is worth more than the bonus.

It is not worth claiming if you are chasing the cash value, if you would not otherwise consider the operator, or if you are handing identity documents to a site you have not checked. Our honest recommendation for most Kiwi players is to ignore no-deposit offers and choose on payout speed and clearable deposit bonuses instead — that is where the real value sits.

Do I pay tax on no deposit bonus winnings in NZ?

No. Inland Revenue treats gambling winnings as the proceeds of chance rather than income, whatever their source. Nothing to declare, nothing to pay, for a recreational player. See tax on gambling winnings in NZ.

Claiming one offer each at genuinely separate operators is permitted. Whether it is a good use of your identity documents is a different question, and we would say generally not.
